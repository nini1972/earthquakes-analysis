While earthquakes rarely "dissolve" solid rock into nothingness, they frequently cause land to drop, slide, or submerge permanently through distinct geological processes:

1. Coseismic Coastal Subsidence (Megathrust Subduction Zones)
Along subduction zones—where one tectonic plate dives beneath another—huge amounts of strain build up over centuries. The upper plate gets compressed and pushed upward like a bent diving board. During a massive earthquake (Magnitude 8.0 to 9.5+), the upper plate snaps back, causing vast areas of coastal land to instantly drop by several meters below sea level.

Cascadia Subduction Zone (Pacific Northwest, USA & Canada): In 1700, a Magnitude ~9.0 earthquake caused miles of coastal forest to instantly drop 1 to 2 meters below sea level. Saltwater rushed in, killing trees and creating "ghost forests" that remain visible today.
Valdivia, Chile (1960 - M9.5): The largest recorded earthquake in history caused vast areas of the Chilean coastline to sink up to 2 meters (6.5 ft), permanently submerging agricultural land into the Pacific Ocean.
Prince William Sound, Alaska (1964 - M9.2): Coastal areas dropped by up to 2.5 meters (8 feet), drowning coastal communities and roads permanently under seawater.
2. Extreme Liquefaction and Lateral Spreading
In saturated, sandy, or reclaimed coastal soils, strong ground motion breaks the friction between soil grains, causing solid ground to act like a fluid. Land can then slide sideways into rivers or the sea (lateral spreading).

Port Royal, Jamaica (1692): Over two-thirds of the famous pirate city of Port Royal—built on loose sand—liquefied during a major earthquake and slid directly into the Caribbean Sea in minutes, where its ruins remain underwater today.
Palu, Indonesia (2018): A M7.5 earthquake triggered severe soil liquefaction, causing entire urban neighborhoods and coastlines to flow into the ocean like mud rivers.
3. Coastal Cliff Collapse & Submarine Landslides
Seismic waves can shatter coastal cliffs or underwater continental slopes, causing large landmasses to drop into deep ocean trenches.

Island Arcs (e.g., Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, Papua New Guinea): Small, low-lying coral islets and river deltas sitting atop steep submarine slopes are particularly vulnerable. Earthquake shaking can trigger submarine landslides that pull island edges into the ocean floor.
High-Risk Regions Worldwide Today
Pacific Northwest (Cascadia): Northern California through Oregon, Washington, and British Columbia.
Indonesian Archipelago & Sunda Trench: High subduction activity combined with loose coastal sediment.
Japan Trench & Nankai Trough: Densely populated coastal planes at risk of coseismic drop and liquefaction.
South Pacific Island Nations (Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, Tonga): Low elevation paired with intense subduction seismicity along the Ring of Fire.
